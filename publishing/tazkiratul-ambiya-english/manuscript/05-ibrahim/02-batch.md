### Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> Broke the Idols

When Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> said to his uncle and his people, "What do you worship? Do you seek false gods besides Allah? What is your opinion of the Lord of the Worlds?" — meaning, if you worship others besides Him, do you think He will leave you without punishment, despite knowing that He alone is the True Benefactor and worthy of worship?

This people had an annual fair. They would go to the jungle, remain engaged in entertainment and sport until evening, and on their way back they would come to the temple, worship the idols, and then return to their affairs. When Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> condemned their idols, they said: "Tomorrow is our festival. There will be a fair in the jungle. We will cook fine food and place it before the idols. Upon returning from the fair, we will eat it as a blessing. You should also come with us and see the splendor of our religion and way, and how we enjoy ourselves." When the day of the fair came, they asked him to come along. The Quran records his response in these words:

> "Then he cast a glance at the stars and said, 'Indeed, I am about to be ill.'"

He looked at the stars as one who calculates the stars looks. Since the people were firm believers in astrology, they understood that perhaps he had calculated from the stars and concluded that he would fall ill. In this way, the people left him and went to their fair.

He <sup>عليه السلام</sup> had already declared before many people: "And by Allah, I will certainly plot against your idols after you have turned your backs."

When the people went to their fair, he considered the opportunity golden — the time had come to put his plan into action. He quietly made his way to the temple. There he saw that the people had placed all kinds of food before the idols to eat upon their return and worship them. He approached the idols and said, "Why do you not eat?" When there was no answer from the idols, he then said to them:

> "What is wrong with you that you do not speak?"

What answer could those lifeless stone statues give? When he saw that these idols could not eat, had no power to speak, and were merely lifeless idols carved by their own hands from stone, he began to strike them.

> "So he broke them into pieces, except the largest of them, that perhaps they might return to it."

The people themselves found out that their idols had been broken. "So they came quickly to him."

> "They said, 'Who has done this to our gods? Indeed, he is a wrongdoer.' Some of them said, 'We heard a youth mentioning them badly, who is called Ibrahim.' They said, 'Then bring him before the eyes of the people, that they may testify.' They said, 'Did you do this to our gods, O Ibrahim?' He said, 'Rather, this largest of them did it. So ask them, if they can speak.' So they turned to themselves and said, 'Indeed, you are the wrongdoers.' Then they were made to turn their heads down: 'You know well that these do not speak.' He said, 'Then do you worship besides Allah that which does not benefit you at all nor harm you? Shame upon you and upon what you worship besides Allah! Then will you not use reason?'"

Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> smashed all the idols to pieces except the largest one, placing an axe on its shoulder. When the people returned, they said, "Someone has broken our gods!" How foolish were the people who still could not understand that an idol that cannot protect itself — how can it help them?

When they asked, "Who has treated our gods this way?" those people before whom Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> had said "I will certainly plot against your idols" told them: "Ibrahim must have done this, because he used to speak ill of our gods." They then said, "Bring Ibrahim forward so that we may establish testimony against him and try him." When Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> was brought, he was asked, "Did you do this?" He said, "The axe is on the shoulder of your chief idol — he must have done it. Ask these gods of yours, if they can speak, they will tell you."

At this wise reply, they paused for a moment to think: "Ibrahim is right, and we are foolish to worship such gods who cannot even protect themselves. This is the truth — we are wronging ourselves in this matter." But after a short while, their misfortune again overtook them, and they said, "We cannot ask them — they do not speak." In this way, they remained firm in their error.

### Objection

When the people were leaving for the fair, they invited Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup>, and he said, "I am ill" — even though he was not ill. This, God forbid, is a lie. Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> broke their idols. When the people asked, he said, "This chief idol did it" — even though the chief idol had not broken the smaller idols. So how could he say this? This, God forbid, also appears to be a lie. In a noble hadith, mention is also made of his three "lies," two of which are these.

### Response

A prophet cannot tell a lie. Lying is a major sin. The noble prophets are free from both minor and major sins, before and after prophethood. That hadith — which some non-Muslims have understood to mean that Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup>, God forbid, told three lies — is explained here so that the true meaning of the noble hadith may be understood. The noble hadith is:

> "Hazrat Abu Huraira <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنه</sup> narrated that the Noble Prophet ﷺ said: 'Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> did not say anything that people considered a lie except three things. Two of these relate to Allah ta'ala's Essence. One is his statement "I am about to be ill," the second is his statement "This chief idol did it," and the third statement (when he was migrating toward Philistine) — one day he and his wife passed by a place where a tyrant oppressor was in control. People told him that a man had arrived with a woman who was more beautiful than all others. The tyrant sent a messenger to Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> to ask who the woman was. In reply, he said, 'She is my sister.' Then he came to Hazrat Saara <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنها</sup> and told her: 'If that tyrant finds out you are my wife, he will forcibly take you from me. If he asks you, tell him you are my sister, because in Islam you are my sister — since there is no believer on the face of the earth besides you and me.' The tyrant sent for Hazrat Saara and called her to him. Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> stood up and began to pray. When Hazrat Saara reached the tyrant, he tried to reach out to her, but he was seized by Allah — he became like a madman, his throat choked, foam came from his mouth, and his heels drummed the ground. He said to Hazrat Saara, 'Pray for me, I will not harm you.' She prayed to Allah, and he recovered. He tried again to reach out, but was seized by Allah ta'ala even more severely. He again asked Hazrat Saara to pray, and she prayed again. When he recovered, he summoned the guard and said, 'You have not brought a human to me — you have brought a jinn.' He gave Hazrat Haajra <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنها</sup> to her as a servant and sent them back."

(Mufti Ahmad Yaar Khan <sup>رحمه الله تعالى</sup> wrote in *Tafseer Naimi* that Hazrat Haajra <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنها</sup> was the daughter of the king of Rome. The tyrant had met the same fate with her, so he said, "Remove these two from here — they are not humans, they are jinn.")

When Hazrat Saara <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنها</sup> returned to Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup>, he was still engaged in prayer. He gestured with his hand to ask how it went. She said, "Allah ta'ala turned the disbeliever's plot back upon his own chest — he was disgraced. He gave me Haajra as a servant." Hazrat Abu Huraira <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنه</sup> said, "O people of Arabia, this (Haajra) is your mother."

Qaazi Iyaaz <sup>رحمه الله تعالى</sup> said: A lie can never be proven from the noble prophets. But these statements that have been mentioned — they are attributed to the listener, who considered them a lie because they appear outwardly to be lies, even though in reality they were not lies. Therefore, the meaning of the Noble Prophet's ﷺ noble command is that Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> spoke in such a manner on three occasions that people considered it a lie. Aside from these three occasions, he never made any statement that people considered a lie.

**The First Noble Statement: "I am about to be ill"**

The meaning of this noble statement has already been explained in detail. When the people invited him to participate in the fair, he looked at the stars and said, "I am about to be ill." He did not say, "I will fall ill tomorrow." He only said, "I am about to be ill." A person does inevitably fall ill at some point in life. He took the distant meaning, while the people understood the near meaning. This is called *tawriya* (ambiguous speech), which is permissible.

A second explanation — Mulla Ali Qaari <sup>رحمه الله تعالى</sup> states: "He said, 'My heart is ill,' because I am very angry that you have made the stars your gods, or that you have made idols your deities. Due to this anger, mental distress is the cause of my heart being ill." That is, his statement was based on truth — he was ill in his heart — but the people could not understand this.

**The Second Statement: "This chief idol did it"**

When the people went to the fair, Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> broke all the idols and placed the axe on the shoulder of the chief idol — the one they considered their greatest god. When the people returned, they said to each other, "Who has committed this outrage against our gods?" Some said, "Ibrahim must have done it." When he was called and asked, he said, "This chief idol did it." The common meaning that comes to mind is that he said, "This chief idol broke them." The people, according to their understanding, considered it a lie. But this is not its meaning at all. Allama Razi <sup>رحمه الله تعالى</sup> has mentioned several possible meanings. What is the actual meaning?

**First:** He did not say, "This idol did it." Rather, by "the chief" he meant himself. His statement was based on irony — that is, the speaker intends one meaning while the listener understands another. This is like when a person skilled in calligraphy writes a beautiful letter, and another who does not know writing asks the master, "Did you write this?" He replies, "Rather, you wrote it" — this is to silence him reproachfully. That is, the statement negates the one who is incapable and affirms the one who is capable. Similarly, Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> attributed the act to the idol to make clear: "Only the one who has the power to do this could have done it. How could the one who has no power do it?"

**Second:** When Hazrat Ibrahim <sup>عليه السلام</sup> saw that the idols had been decorated and the people considered the chief idol specially chosen, he became angry. Seeing that the chief idol was more decorated and they revered it more, his anger intensified. When he saw this chief idol, his anger grew more severe, so he broke all the idols. He attributed his action to the idol because it was the cause of the breaking and disgrace — seeing it had made him angrier. Just as an action is attributed to the doer, it is also attributed to the one who incites the action.

**Third:** He spoke according to their religion. "When you consider this a god, then it must have done it. The one you consider worthy of worship — who is your deity — should be capable of this action, indeed, should have even greater power than this." The purpose was to make them understand: "When you do not believe that the idol broke the others because it has no power to break, then how can it be a deity?"

**Fourth:** The actual wording is: "Whoever was to do it has done it. This is your chief — ask him." The purpose of this statement was: "I have done this deed. Ask your chief god — if it has the power to speak." In this too, there was preaching to them: "Your god cannot even tell you who did this deed."

**Fifth:** There is a pause after *kabeeruhum* (their chief), and the speech begins anew from *haza* (this). The meaning is: "The deed was done by their chief. This is your god — ask it, if it speaks." By "their chief," he meant himself, because a human is greater than all idols. The purpose was: "The deed was done by their chief — that chief is I, because I am a human and greater than your gods. What status do these idols have compared to a human?"

**Sixth:** There is precedence and delay in the speech. The actual meaning is: "He said, 'Their chief did it — if they can speak, then ask them.'" The attribution of the deed to their chief idol is conditional: "If they speak, then their chief did it. Since they do not speak, their chief did not do it."

**Seventh:** In one recitation, it appears as *فَعَلَهُ كَبِيرُهُمْ* (perhaps their chief did it), meaning "Perhaps the doer is their chief."

From all these explanations, it becomes clear that Hazrat Ibrahim's <sup>عليه السلام</sup> statement was based on truth, even if the listeners could not understand it and, in their false assumption, considered it a lie.

### It Is Better to Call the Narrators Liars Than to Call the Noble Prophets Liars

Allama Razi <sup>رحمه الله تعالى</sup> writes at this point: "If there is any narration which proves that a noble prophet is a liar, and no interpretation can be found to establish the truthfulness of the noble prophet, then in that case the narrators can be called liars, but calling the noble prophets liars is impossible."

In such a case, the narration will be rejected, but no difference will be allowed in the honor of the noble prophets.

### His Third Statement

Regarding Hazrat Saara <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنها</sup>, he said, "She is my sister." The reason is made clear in the noble hadith itself: he did not mean that she was his biological sister. Rather, he said to Hazrat Saara <sup>رضي الله تعالى عنها</sup>, "In Islam, you are my sister," because from the perspective of Islamic brotherhood, even a father and son are brothers, a mother and son are brother and sister, and similarly a husband and wife are brother and sister to each other.
